Nordmarka: Oslo's Forest at the End of the Metro

A huge forest of spruce, lakes and trails begins where Oslo's northern suburbs end. Here is what Nordmarka is, how to get there, and how to enjoy it like a local — in any season.

Home › Journal › Nordmarka: Oslo's Forest at the End of the Metro

21 October 2026 · 6 min read · By Oslo Hiking Tours

Most capital cities have parks. Oslo has a forest. Nordmarka starts at the end of the metro line and stretches north for kilometre after kilometre of spruce, lakes, hills and trails. For people in Oslo it is the backyard: where they ski on winter weekends, swim in summer and pick berries in autumn. For visitors it is the easiest way to see real Norwegian nature without leaving the city.

What is Nordmarka?

The forest that surrounds Oslo is called Oslomarka, or simply Marka, and Nordmarka is its largest and best-known part, to the north of the city. It is protected by its own law, the Marka Act, which limits building and keeps the forest open for outdoor life. Inside it there are hundreds of lakes, a dense network of paths and gravel roads, and in winter hundreds of kilometres of groomed ski tracks.

How to get there

You don't need a car. Two metro lines run straight to the edge of the forest, both on an ordinary Ruter zone 1 ticket:

  • Line 5 to Sognsvann — about 20 minutes from the centre. The station is a few minutes' walk from Sognsvann lake, the most popular gateway to the forest.
  • Line 1 to Frognerseteren — about 25–30 minutes, climbing past Holmenkollen to almost 500 metres above sea level, with views over the city and fjord. Snow is more reliable up here in winter.

The right to roam

Norway has a tradition called allemannsretten — the right to roam. You may walk freely in the forest, pick berries and mushrooms, and pitch a tent for a night or two at least 150 metres from the nearest house. With it comes responsibility: leave no trace, take your rubbish home, and respect wildlife and other people. Campfires are banned in and near forests from 15 April to 15 September, but allowed in winter when it is safe.

Cabins and cafés

Part of the charm of Nordmarka is its serving cabins — old timber lodges deep in the forest where you can buy waffles, coffee and hot food. Ullevålseter, a 30–45 minute walk from Sognsvann, is a classic. Frognerseteren, at the top of line 1, is a historic restaurant with a famous apple cake and a view over the city. Opening days vary through the year, so check before you set out.

Nordmarka through the seasons

  • Winter (December–March): snow, frozen lakes, ski tracks and short, golden days. The forest is at its most magical.
  • Spring: a muddy in-between season as the snow melts — lovely late spring days, but trails can be wet.
  • Summer: swimming in the lakes, long light evenings and endless walking.
  • Autumn: blueberries, mushrooms, and birch and aspen turning yellow among the dark spruce.

Winter etiquette: stay out of the ski tracks

In winter, many of Nordmarka's paths become groomed cross-country ski tracks. Locals take these seriously. If you're walking, keep to the side, never step in the parallel grooves of a classic ski track, and step aside when skiers come past. Snowshoeing works best off the tracks altogether, in the deeper snow among the trees.

Where to start

For a first visit, take line 5 to Sognsvann and walk the flat loop around the lake — about three kilometres, and easy in any season. For more, follow the signposted paths further into the forest, or let someone show you the way.
