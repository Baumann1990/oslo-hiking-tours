Can You See the Northern Lights in Oslo?

The honest answer, why it is so rare this far south, how to give yourself a small chance — and where to go if you really want to see them.

Home › Journal › Can You See the Northern Lights in Oslo?

11 November 2026 · 5 min read · By Oslo Hiking Tours

It is one of the most common questions visitors ask us in winter, and the honest answer is: rarely. Oslo is a wonderful winter city, but it is not a northern lights destination. Here is why — and what you can do instead.

Why the northern lights are rare in Oslo

The northern lights appear in a ring around the magnetic north pole, known as the auroral oval. On a normal night that ring sits over northern Norway — places like Tromsø, Senja and Lofoten, well north of the Arctic Circle. Oslo lies at about 60° north, roughly 1,000 kilometres further south, so on most nights the aurora is simply out of reach below the horizon.

On top of that, Oslo is a bright city. Even when there is faint activity to the north, light pollution from the city and its suburbs washes it out.

When it does happen

A few times a year — sometimes more in years of high solar activity — a strong geomagnetic storm pushes the auroral oval far enough south to be seen from southern Norway. On those rare nights, the lights have been seen and photographed from Oslo. These nights are hard to predict more than a day or two in advance, so you cannot plan a trip around one.

How to give yourself a chance

  • Watch the forecast. Aurora apps and space-weather forecasts give the geomagnetic activity level (the Kp index). In Oslo, you generally need a strong storm before anything is visible.
  • Check the cloud cover. A clear sky matters as much as solar activity. Yr, the Norwegian weather service, shows cloud cover hour by hour.
  • Get away from the lights. Take the metro up into Nordmarka — to Sognsvann or Frognerseteren — and walk a little way from the station lights. A frozen lake with an open view north is ideal.
  • Look north, low on the horizon. In Oslo the aurora usually appears as a pale glow or faint pillars low in the northern sky, not overhead.
  • Use your phone camera. Cameras pick up colour the eye cannot. A faint grey glow often shows green and pink in a long-exposure photo.

Where to go for reliable northern lights

If seeing the aurora is a priority, fly north. Tromsø, Senja and Lofoten are about two hours by plane from Oslo, and from late September to late March the lights are visible there on most clear, dark nights. Our sister company in Senja, Senja Fjord Tours, runs private northern lights cruises on the fjord, away from all light pollution.

A dark winter evening in Oslo, without the aurora

What Oslo does offer is a real winter forest right at the end of the metro line. On a clear winter evening, walking through the snowy spruce by headlamp to a crackling fire, with the stars overhead and the city glowing below, is a very Norwegian way to spend the dark hours. That is exactly what our Campfire Evening Walk is — and on the rare night the sky lights up, you'll already be in the right place.
