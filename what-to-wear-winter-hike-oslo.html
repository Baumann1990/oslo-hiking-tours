What to Wear for a Winter Hike in Oslo

A simple layering system, the boots question, and the one cheap item every visitor should buy — so you stay warm in the forest above Oslo without packing like a polar explorer.

Home › Journal › What to Wear for a Winter Hike in Oslo

4 November 2026 · 5 min read · By Oslo Hiking Tours

You don't need expensive expedition gear to enjoy a winter hike in Oslo. Temperatures in the forest above the city are usually somewhere between a few degrees above zero and minus ten, sometimes colder. What matters is how you dress: in layers, with warm feet and a way to stay upright on ice.

The three-layer system

Norwegians dress for the outdoors in layers, so they can add or remove warmth as they go. Walking uphill you'll get warm quickly; standing still for a break you'll cool down just as fast.

  • Base layer: long-sleeved top and long johns in merino wool or a synthetic fabric. This is the most important layer — it keeps you dry, and dry means warm.
  • Mid layer: a fleece, wool sweater or light insulated jacket. Bring an extra one in your bag for breaks.
  • Outer layer: a windproof, ideally waterproof, jacket and trousers. Wind is what makes cold feel brutal.

Avoid cotton — jeans, cotton hoodies and cotton socks absorb sweat and stay wet and cold. It is the most common mistake we see.

Feet: the part that matters most

Cold feet end a winter walk faster than anything else. Wear warm, waterproof winter boots with a good sole, a size that leaves room for thick socks. Ordinary trainers will be soaked within minutes. Pair them with wool socks, and bring a spare pair in a plastic bag.

Snowshoes strap onto your own boots, so the same advice applies when there's deep snow.

Ice spikes: the one thing to buy

Oslo's pavements and forest paths are often icy in winter, especially around freeze-thaw days. Slip-on ice spikes — brodder in Norwegian — stretch over your shoes and make a huge difference. Sports shops and many supermarkets in Oslo sell them. You'll use them in the city too.

Hands, head and neck

  • A warm hat that covers your ears.
  • Gloves or mittens — mittens are warmer. A thin liner glove underneath lets you use your phone.
  • A buff or scarf for wind on the face.

What to bring in your daypack

  • A thermos with something hot — in Norway, a thermos is standard winter kit.
  • A snack: chocolate, nuts, or a sandwich. The cold burns energy.
  • An extra warm layer for stops.
  • Your phone, kept in an inside pocket — cold drains batteries quickly. A small power bank helps.
  • A headlamp if there's any chance you'll be out after 15:00 in December or January.
  • Sunglasses on bright days — sun on snow is surprisingly strong.
